Split vote

Teaching copyright divided reviewers more than any other category

"Teaches about copyright" got the most low scores, four 2s, and its scores spread almost evenly from 2 to 5. Reviewers of the same game often disagreed. Copyright Carnival got both a 5 and a 2 for teaching.

Reading the comments, the high scores go to games where you have to know a copyright idea to win. In Escape the Vault, the answers are the keys. In Fair Use Tac-Toe, a wrong answer costs you your score. When learning copyright is how you win, reviewers noticed.

How the 19 reviewers scored each category

37%

of reviews found clear, detailed AI documentation

Almost every game used AI. But in 10 of 19 reviews, the reviewer could not find any clear note about how AI was used. Only 7 found a link to an AI Documentation Doc or chat log.

Why this matters: reviewers of the same game often gave different answers. Chef's Copyright Cafe was rated "detailed" once and "little to none" twice. If players can't find your AI notes, they don't count. One reviewer even wrote "cite what AI you used" as their suggestion.

"what I liked" comments were about learning copyright

When reviewers said what they liked, they talked about the game itself: portals, boosts, moves counters, multiplayer, smooth animation. That's a real compliment to how well these games were built.

Their suggestions point the same way. The most common asks were more challenge and a real opponent, together in 9 of 19 suggestions. Another 4 asked for more or clearer copyright questions.

Also worth knowing: games built in Canva and games coded with AI scored almost exactly the same overall (3.84 vs. 3.85). The tool did not decide the result. The design choices did.

Level up for next time

Three goals for the whole class, straight from what your reviewers asked for.

Make copyright the key

Put a copyright question right where the player needs it to win, like the keys in Escape the Vault. Don't add questions on top of the game. Build the game around them.

Give players a reason to replay

Harder levels, a real opponent, surprises that cost points. Reviewers asked for challenge and competition more than anything else.

Show your AI work where players can see it

Put a clear link to your AI Documentation Doc or chat log right on the game page, with a sentence about what AI made and what you made.

About this data. 19 peer reviews of 8 games, collected September 25 and 29, 2026, with a Google Form built from the class rubric. Each review scored four categories from 1 to 5. One test entry that was missing a score was removed. Games with only one or two reviews are marked, since a single review can swing an average a lot. Student names are left out on purpose. Every number on this page was recalculated from the raw form responses.
